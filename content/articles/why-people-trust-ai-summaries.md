## In short

- A BBC and Ipsos study surveyed 2,000 UK adults. 64% instinctively agreed it is important to watch for errors in AI content. Only 38% instinctively said they would question an AI-written summary.
- The study cites BBC and EBU research that found at least one significant error in 45% of leading assistants' news answers.
- An experiment with about 900 people found that AI help raised accuracy on questions about videos. With a deliberately wrong assistant, accuracy fell by up to 32%.
- Both studies point to the same habits: look at the source, and prefer answers that show where they come from.

Most of us now meet AI answers before we meet the thing they summarise. Two studies, one by a broadcaster and one by universities, look at what that does to readers. They also look at what helps.

**What people say and what they do.** The BBC commissioned Ipsos to survey 2,000 UK adults in May 2025 and to run six online workshops with 36 people. The report says that just over a third of UK adults trust AI to produce accurate summaries, rising to half of under-35s. It describes "passive trust, where confident, well-written output is taken at face value rather than interrogated". People scan for cues such as dates, figures and named sources, it says. When those are present, the answer feels credible.

The chart above shows the gap between saying and doing. In the workshops, when participants were told a summary contained an error, many said they would check elsewhere. Some went back to skimming when a later summary looked clean and named recognisable outlets, the report adds.

When an error does show, it matters. 84% said a factual error would have a major impact on their trust in an AI summary. The figures were 76% for a sourcing or attribution error, 81% for an opinion presented as fact and 73% for an opinion the AI added itself. People blamed the news outlet named in a summary as well as the tool, the report found.

**What happens when the answer is wrong.** A paper by Anders Giovanni Møller, Elisa Bassignana, Francesco Pierri and Luca Maria Aiello tested the same habit. It was first posted to arXiv on 20 March 2026 and revised on 31 August 2026. About 900 participants answered more than 8,000 questions about videos. There were three conditions: the video alone, the video with an AI assistant, and the video with an assistant designed to give false answers.

The first result is good news. AI help raised accuracy by 3 to 7% when people had watched the relevant part of the video. When they had not, it raised accuracy by 27 to 35%. The second result is the warning. With the deceiving assistant, accuracy fell by up to 32%. The participants' confidence in their answers stayed about the same in all three conditions. On long videos, up to 51.3% of participants accepted the deceiving assistant's answer without checking it against the video. Only 7.9 to 15.8% asked the AI and then checked its answer against the video.

Watching the video helped. In the last round, the deceiving assistant replaced the regular one. Accuracy then fell to around 20% for questions answered without watching the relevant segment. For people who did watch it, the drop against the video-only condition was smaller: 9.4 points for short videos and 5.1 for long ones.

**What helps.** The authors write that designing systems "that encourage appropriate levels of trust, critical verification, and transparency will be essential". The BBC report is more specific. It asks for clear provenance: publisher, author, publication time, update notes and working links. It asks for what is known to be kept apart from conjecture, for quotes kept verbatim and for viewpoints attributed to named people. For a reader, the plain version is to open the source behind a summary before relying on a figure, a date or a quote.

## What this does not claim

- **The two studies are different.** One is a UK survey and set of workshops about news summaries. The other is an experiment about questions on videos. Neither shows how often any one assistant is wrong today.
- **The 45% is not this newsroom's finding.** It is the BBC and EBU's result, which the BBC and Ipsos report cites. The report itself was read. The BBC and EBU study was not read here.
- **The experiment used a deliberately wrong assistant.** It shows how people respond when an assistant is wrong, not how often real assistants are.
- **The paper was read in its arXiv version.** Its arXiv page says it was accepted at EMNLP 2026. Its authors say its participants were US and UK crowdworkers who may not represent everyone. They also say it used a single AI model.
- **"Instinctively agree" is the survey's measure** of how fast people agreed, not what they did.
- **The advice on what helps is the studies' own.** This newsroom has not tested it.
