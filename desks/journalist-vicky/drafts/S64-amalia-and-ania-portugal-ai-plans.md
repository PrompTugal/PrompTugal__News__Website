## In short

- The government says its National AI Agenda (ANIA) has four areas of action and 32 initiatives, and that it came into force when it was published.
- AMALIA is a language model for European Portuguese, presented on 1 July 2026 and free to use. Five universities built it, with 5.5 million euros from the Recovery and Resilience Plan.
- Its first planned use is in public administration.
- The sources disagree on how many researchers worked on AMALIA: 80 or about 60.

## The story

Portugal has an AI plan and a piece of AI software, both official.

**The plan.** The National Artificial Intelligence Agenda was approved by Council of Ministers Resolution No. 2/2026, the government's digital portal says. It runs with an action plan for 2026 to 2030 and aims to put Portugal "at the forefront of AI in Europe", the portal says.

It has four areas of action: infrastructure and data, innovation and adoption, talent and skills, and responsibility and ethics. Its 32 initiatives involve universities, research centres, companies and public administration. The portal gives no budget.

**The software.** AMALIA stands for Automatic Multimodal Language Assistant with Artificial Intelligence. It is a large language model, a program trained on very large amounts of text so that it can read and write it. Chatbots run on such models. INESC-ID, a research institute, calls AMALIA the first one developed specifically for European Portuguese.

The government's explainer says a base version came in September 2025 and the final version on 1 July 2026, at the Técnico Innovation Center in Lisbon. The Prime Minister attended, INESC-ID and NOVA FCT, a university faculty, say.

NOVA FCT names the consortium: itself, Instituto Superior Técnico, and the universities of Coimbra, Minho and Porto. INESC-ID says 80 researchers took part. NOVA FCT and the government's explainer say about 60. Both put the funding at 5.5 million euros from the Recovery and Resilience Plan, the programme through which Portugal spends EU recovery funds.

AMALIA is open source, which means anyone can read, use and change it. The project's page on Hugging Face, a site where AI models are shared, lists a version under the Apache 2.0 licence. The government's explainer says public bodies, businesses, universities and citizens can use it free of charge.

The authors' technical report, posted on arXiv, a site where researchers share papers, says how it was built. They started from EuroLLM-9B, an existing open model, and trained it on 5.8 billion tokens of European Portuguese. Tokens are the pieces of text, roughly words or parts of words, that a model reads. The text came from Arquivo.pt, the Portuguese Web Archive, filtered and with personal data removed. The authors say AMALIA matches similar open models on translated tests and does better on tests made for European Portuguese.

Public administration comes first. The government's explainer lists assistants for public services and the gov.pt portal, document analysis, research support and teaching tools.

The model page carries a warning. The model can state false things, and it should not make high-stakes decisions without a qualified person overseeing it.

Researchers, public bodies and developers who work in Portuguese could start with AMALIA's model page. Companies and universities can look for their place in ANIA's 32 initiatives.

## What this does not claim

- **ANIA and AMALIA are not linked here.** Neither source says AMALIA is one of ANIA's 32 initiatives.
- **These are aims, not results.** The uses for AMALIA are expectations, and ANIA's effects are goals. The newsroom did not read ANIA's action plan.
- **The researcher counts are not reconciled.** Each body gives its own.
- **The test results are the authors' own.** The newsroom did not read the results tables or try the model.
- **Which version does what is unclear.** The government's explainer calls the final version multimodal, meaning it handles text, voice, images and video. The model page the newsroom read describes a text model.
