## In short

- On 11 November 2026, the European Commission proposed a Digital Fairness Act, a major update of EU consumer law for shopping online.
- It would ban manipulative "dark patterns", make subscriptions easier to cancel and give consumers a right to reach a human in customer service.
- It also sets rules for AI agents that buy on a consumer's behalf. On first use, the consumer must choose whether to confirm every purchase, only some, or none.
- An agent would have to disclose any payment or conflict of interest behind a suggested purchase, and the consumer would then have to confirm it.
- It is a proposal. The European Parliament and the Council must agree it before it becomes law.

## The story

The [European Commission](../companies/european-commission/index.html) wants consumers to be as well protected online as offline. Its proposed Digital Fairness Act would update EU consumer law, the Commission says, alongside new powers for the Commission to investigate and fine in large cross-border cases.

Most of the proposal targets familiar problems. It would ban dark patterns, such as fake claims that a product is almost sold out. Prices paid in in-app currencies would have to be clear. Consumers could switch off addictive design features, and gambling-like features such as pay-to-win would be banned for minors. Influencers would have to be open about paid content. Subscriptions would be easier to cancel, free trials would need active confirmation before turning into paid contracts, and consumers could ask to talk to a human in customer service when other means fail.

The newest part is about AI agents. The proposal calls them "automated systems" that can conclude and perform contracts "on the consumers' behalf". It notes that such systems, including AI, are becoming increasingly relevant for traders and consumers. Article 26 sets out what a trader offering such an agent would have to do.

On first use, the consumer must expressly choose one of three settings: confirm all purchases the agent proposes, confirm only certain ones, based on criteria such as a maximum price and a time limit, or confirm none. The choice is sent to the consumer on a durable medium, and it can be changed at any time with easy tools. When the consumer confirms, the agent must show the key information consumer law already requires.

The agent must also disclose any payment received for proposing a contract, and any conflict of interest. In that case the consumer must confirm the purchase, whatever setting they chose. Traders could not force consumers to use an agent to shop on their sites. Consumer rights stay in place even if the consumer pre-approves nothing, the proposal says. Agents and traders could identify themselves through the planned European Business Wallets or the European Digital Identity Wallet. Financial services are excluded from these rules.

The Commission says the package could save EU consumers "up to €13.7 billion a year". The Digital Fairness Act would apply 12 months after it enters into force, according to the draft. The European Parliament and the Council will now discuss it.

Startups at Web Summit building shopping agents, or the payment and identity layers under them, may want to read Article 26 closely: it sketches the controls an agent would need in the EU.

## What this does not claim

- **A proposal, not law.** The text may change in the European Parliament and the Council. Nothing in it applies yet.
- **The €13.7 billion.** It is the Commission's own estimate, for the package as a whole. We have not checked it.
- **"AI agent" is our word.** The proposal speaks of "automated systems", which may or may not use AI.
