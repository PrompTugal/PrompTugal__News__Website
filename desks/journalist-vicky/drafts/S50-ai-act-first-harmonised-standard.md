## In short

- The European Commission has published in the EU's Official Journal the reference to EN 18286:2026, the first harmonised standard supporting the AI Act.
- It describes a quality management system for providers of high-risk AI systems, built around the AI system's lifecycle.
- Providers who apply it are presumed to meet the AI Act's Article 17 requirements that it covers, the Commission says.
- Using it is voluntary. More harmonised standards are expected.
- The rules for high-risk AI in sensitive areas, such as education and employment, apply from 2 December 2027.

## The story

The EU's AI Act now has its first harmonised standard. The [European Commission](../companies/european-commission/index.html) has published its reference in the Official Journal, through Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/2274. The standard is EN 18286:2026, "Artificial intelligence - Quality management system for EU AI Act regulatory purposes". It was developed by CEN and CENELEC, two of Europe's standards bodies.

A harmonised standard is a practical route to meeting part of the law. Article 17 of the AI Act says providers of high-risk AI systems must have a documented quality management system. It lists what that system must cover, from design and testing to data management and who is responsible for what. EN 18286 turns those requirements into a working system, the Commission says. A provider who applies it is presumed to comply with the Article 17 requirements it covers.

The standard is built around the AI system's lifecycle rather than the organisation, according to the Commission. It is meant to sit on top of standards that companies may already use, such as ISO/IEC 42001, ISO 9001 or a sector's own system, such as ISO 13485 for medical devices. It does not replace them. Providers in regulated sectors can combine their management systems instead of running separate ones. Organisations without ISO/IEC 42001 can use it to extend an existing system or build a new one.

Using a harmonised standard is voluntary. Providers can show they comply in other ways, the Commission says, but a standard referenced in the Official Journal gives legal certainty. The AI Act also says the system should be proportionate to the size of the provider, especially for small and medium-sized companies and start-ups. They must still meet the law's required level of rigour.

The timing matters. The AI Act has applied since 2 August 2026, with exceptions. After a political agreement known as the AI Omnibus, the rules for high-risk AI in sensitive areas apply from 2 December 2027. These areas include biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, migration and border control. For AI built into products such as lifts or toys, they apply from 2 August 2028. The Commission calls EN 18286 an important milestone ahead of those dates, with more standards to follow. It has asked CEN and CENELEC for standards in ten areas, including risk management, data quality, human oversight and cybersecurity.

For European AI companies at Web Summit that build systems which may count as high-risk, this is a concrete tool with a deadline. Founders working on AI for hiring, education or infrastructure could compare notes with the EU's experts. The Commission planned a "Building AI in Europe" masterclass at the summit on regulatory guidance and support, but it was set for Wednesday, the day Web Summit called off.
