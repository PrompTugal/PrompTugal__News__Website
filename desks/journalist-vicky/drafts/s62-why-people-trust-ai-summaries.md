## In short

- A BBC and Ipsos study of 2,000 UK adults found that 64% agree it is important to watch for errors in AI content, but only 38% say they would question an AI-written summary.
- The study cites BBC and EBU research that found at least one significant error in 45% of leading assistants' news answers.
- An experiment with about 900 people on video questions found AI help raised accuracy, and that people followed a deliberately wrong assistant, with accuracy falling by up to 32%.
- Both studies point to the same habits: look at the source, and prefer answers that show where they come from.

Most of us now meet AI answers before we meet the thing they summarise. Two pieces of research, one from a broadcaster and one from universities, look at what that does to the reader, and what seems to help.

**What people say and what they do.** The BBC commissioned Ipsos to survey 2,000 UK adults in May 2025 and to run six online workshops with 36 people. The report says that just over a third of UK adults trust AI to produce accurate summaries, rising to half of under-35s. It describes "passive trust, where confident, well-written output is taken at face value rather than interrogated". People scan for cues such as dates, figures and named sources, it says, and when they are present the answer feels credible.

The gap shows in the numbers. Of the people surveyed, 64% instinctively agreed that it is important to watch out for errors in AI content, but only 38% instinctively said they would question the information in an AI-generated summary. In the workshops, people who had been shown an error said they would check elsewhere. The report adds that when a later summary looked clean and named recognisable outlets, some went back to skimming.

When an error does show, it matters. 84% said a factual error would have a major impact on their trust in an AI summary, 76% said the same of a sourcing or attribution error, 81% of an opinion presented as fact, and 73% of an opinion the AI added itself. The report also found people blame the news outlet named in a summary as well as the tool.

**What happens when the answer is wrong.** A paper posted to arXiv on 20 March 2026 by Anders Giovanni Møller, Elisa Bassignana, Francesco Pierri and Luca Maria Aiello tested the same habit. About 900 participants answered more than 8,000 questions about videos, in three conditions: the video alone, the video with an AI assistant, and the video with an assistant designed to give false answers.

The first result is good news. AI help raised accuracy by 3 to 7% when people had watched the relevant part of the video, and by 27 to 35% when they had not. The second is the warning. With the deceiving assistant, accuracy fell by up to 32%, and the participants' confidence in their answers stayed about the same in all three conditions. In the long-video condition, up to 51.3% of participants skipped the video and relied on the AI alone. Only 7.9 to 15.8% asked the AI and then checked its answer against the video.

Checking worked. In the last round, when the deceiving assistant replaced the regular one, accuracy fell to around 20% for questions answered without watching the relevant segment. For people who did engage with the video, the drop against the video-only condition was smaller: 9.4 points for short videos and 5.1 for long ones.

**What helps.** The authors write that designing systems "that encourage appropriate levels of trust, critical verification, and transparency will be essential". The BBC report is more specific about what makes a summary sturdier for readers: clear provenance (publisher, author, publication time, update notes and working links), separating what is known from what is conjecture, keeping quotes verbatim, and attributing viewpoints to named people. For a reader, the plain version is to open the source behind a summary, especially for a figure, a date or a quote, before relying on it.

## What this does not claim

- **The two studies are different.** One is a UK survey and set of workshops about news summaries; the other is an experiment about questions on videos. Neither shows how often any one assistant is wrong today.
- **The 45% is not this newsroom's finding.** It is the BBC and EBU's result, which the BBC and Ipsos report cites. The report itself was read; the BBC and EBU study was not read here.
- **The experiment used a deliberately wrong assistant.** It shows how people respond when an assistant is wrong, not how often real assistants are.
- **The arXiv paper is a preprint,** and its authors say its participants were US and UK crowdworkers who may not represent everyone, and that it used a single AI model. This newsroom has not seen it peer reviewed.
- **"Instinctively agree" is the survey's measure** of how fast people agreed, not what they did.
- **The advice on what helps is the studies' own.** This newsroom has not tested it.
